# WibbleJelly Games - house voice

Use this guide for marketing and editorial copy. Do not apply it to legal
policies, product dimensions, safety instructions, prices, contact details,
form labels, or anything where a loose phrase could cost someone money.

## Facts first

The captured WordPress site in `capture/` and the rebuilt pages are the
evidence. Do not invent customers, reviews, team members, lead times, or
claims about a game. If a claim is not supported by the existing site, leave
it out or flag it for Nick to confirm.

Supported company facts:

- WibbleJelly Games is the trading name of WibbleJelly Ltd, registered in
  England No. 6951967, based at Wayside Cottage, Spittal, Fangfoss, York,
  YO41 5QR.
- It is a family-run Yorkshire business, founded by Nick Forbes in 2015.
- It designs and manufactures original games for the activities hire
  industry. The games are compact, easy to set up, and economical to run.
- The workshop has a laser cutter, CNC router, 3D printer, lathe, and a
  pick-and-place machine for PCBs. The background is electronics and
  software engineering.
- The company does not import completed games and pass them off as its own.
- New games are tested at Scout camps and jamborees, including events with
  thousands of young people, before going on sale.
- Every game sold is supported by the company. The guarantee is 12 months,
  return to base. Games are built to order, aimed at dispatch within 10
  working days.
- Custom game design and building is offered. There is no retail outlet;
  the company attends trade shows including INPAS and the Leisure Supplies
  Show.

## The voice

Nick explaining the job to someone at a hire trade show: dry, plain,
specific, and more interested in how the machine is made than in selling a
lifestyle. Yorkshire, without performing a Yorkshire accent.

The machines are fun. The copy does not have to perform that for them.

- Use "I" for Nick's own history and workshop decisions. Use "we" for the
  family business and what it sells. Do not invent a team or a job title.
- Prefer specifics: a flight case, a pick-and-place machine, a game that
  survived a week of Scouts, a laser-cut panel. Avoid "exciting
  experiences", "solutions", "bespoke journeys", and similar catalogue
  language.
- Use plain words and complete sentences. A little looseness and the
  occasional hedge sound more credible than polished parallel slogans.
- The WhatsApp test: could Nick type this on his phone in the workshop? If
  it sounds like a brochure, take the polish off while keeping the facts.
- Trust the reader. Do not explain a joke, justify every sentence, or repeat
  the conclusion.

Voice references are for plainness and craft specificity, not for lines or
punchlines: Fred Dibnah, ordinary conversations at a Scout camp or trade
show, and the dry timing of a good Yorkshire storyteller.

## Patterns to avoid

- Fragments used as closers: "The lot." "No messing."
- Cinematic summaries: "The rest is history."
- Rule-of-three slogans and a comic third item in every list.
- "Not X, not Y" constructions.
- Repeated tag questions.
- Deliberately trying to be funny.
- Generic Yorkshire markers instead of real detail.
- Em dashes or double hyphens. Use a single spaced hyphen for an aside.
- Marketing intensifiers: "artisanal", "curated", "elevate", "unleash",
  "seamless", "game-changing", "cutting-edge", "passionate", "premium
  experiences", "solutions".

## No-go words

Avoid outside direct quotations:

- owt / nowt
- our kid
- champion as an adjective
- ay up / by 'eck / ee bah gum
- proper or dead as generic intensifiers
- me in place of my
- phonetic accent spelling
- honest as the day is long
- salt of the earth
- no nonsense

## Editing checklist

1. Read the existing page or capture before changing it. Keep product names,
   dimensions, and the original facts.
2. Record what the page already says. Do not reduce the useful detail.
3. Rewrite only the copy that needs it, in plain English.
4. Read it aloud. Mark anything that sounds like a slogan.
5. Check every claim against `capture/` or another current source.
6. Keep legal, technical, safety, pricing, and contact text literal.
7. Recheck headings, alt text, links, and the footer details.
